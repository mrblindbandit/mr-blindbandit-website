Research Essay / KAELEB SAVON HECK

Building a Career in Music: The Work Behind Mr. Blindbandit

A researched look at the creative, technical and business work behind a professional independent music career.

Author
Kaeleb Savon Heck
School
Penn Foster High School
Course
English
Date
12 September 2026

A finished song may last only a few minutes, but a career in music is built from hundreds of hours that listeners never hear. Behind every release are decisions about writing, recording, editing, promotion, technology, contracts, scheduling, and audience development. That combination is what attracts me to the career I am already building as Mr. Blindbandit, an independent musician, producer, digital creator, and founder of Blindbandit Records. Being born completely blind has shaped how I work, but it has not reduced the size of the career I want. Instead, it has pushed me to learn accessible technology and to treat sound as both an art form and a practical way of understanding the world. A professional music career fits my interests because it combines creativity, independence, technology, and entrepreneurship; succeeding in it requires continuous training, disciplined daily work, strong promotion, and the ability to adapt to an industry in which income and opportunities can change quickly.

The public usually sees the most visible part of a musician's job: the performance or finished recording. The actual work is much broader. The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics explains that musicians perform for audiences and recordings, practice, rehearse, book venues, and promote themselves through websites, social media, interviews, and other publicity (Bureau of Labor Statistics). O*NET adds tasks such as learning new music, participating in studio recordings, arranging music, collaborating with managers, and promoting music through media activities (O*NET OnLine). For an independent artist, many of these responsibilities fall on the same person. A normal week can include developing a song, recording, reviewing mixes, communicating with collaborators, updating a website, planning a release, answering business messages, and posting content. Running Blindbandit Records adds another layer because a label involves organizing projects and building a professional identity. My official press page describes my work as music, production, video, and digital storytelling, showing why I view this career as more than simply performing songs (Mr. Blindbandit).

There is no single educational route into professional music. The Bureau of Labor Statistics states that musicians and singers typically do not need a formal postsecondary credential, although classical and opera performers may pursue bachelor's or advanced degrees. Across genres, however, extensive training and regular practice are essential; business courses such as marketing can also help because self-promotion is part of the profession (Bureau of Labor Statistics). O*NET shows the same mixture of artistic and practical abilities. Its profile lists active listening, speaking, monitoring, reading comprehension, and critical thinking among important skills, while the work requires creativity, planning, accuracy, and cooperation (O*NET OnLine). For me, training is not limited to a classroom. I learn by creating, experimenting with production tools, studying how releases are presented, working with others, and improving the systems I use to manage my music. Formal education can be valuable, but learning continues long after any course ends. I would especially benefit from targeted study in music production, copyright basics, marketing, accounting, and artist management. Those subjects would strengthen the parts of the career that are easy to overlook when the focus stays only on creativity. The more I understand the business behind a release, the better prepared I am to make decisions for both my own work and Blindbandit Records.

Accessibility is a major part of my training. I was born blind with Leber congenital amaurosis, so I do not work by looking at visual waveforms or watching a screen. I use screen readers, audio feedback, keyboard commands, memory, and careful listening to move through digital tools. On my website, I describe the difference simply: "What other people look at, I listen to" (Heck). Some software is built around visual controls, which can require a different workflow; at the same time, relying on sound has made close listening central to how I create. My press materials explain that I use accessible technology and screen readers while working across ambient, cinematic, electronic, and hip-hop sounds (Mr. Blindbandit). Accessibility is therefore not separate from my career. It shapes how I organize files, operate software, communicate online, evaluate sound, and solve problems. Learning to work independently with accessible tools is part of my professional training.

The financial side of a music career is less predictable than a traditional salary. The Bureau of Labor Statistics reports a median hourly wage of $47.80 for musicians and singers in May 2025, but nearly half of workers in the occupation were self-employed, and federal wage data do not include the self-employed. Part-time and intermittent work is also common (Bureau of Labor Statistics). The median figure therefore should not be read as a guaranteed full-time income. Independent musicians may earn from several activities instead of one steady paycheck. The job outlook is competitive as well: employment is projected to show little or no overall change from 2025 to 2035, although about 17,400 openings are expected each year as workers leave the occupation or move into other jobs (Bureau of Labor Statistics). These numbers show why creativity must be matched with discipline, promotion, financial planning, and the willingness to create multiple opportunities.

Location can influence opportunities, but technology has changed what location means for an independent artist. The Bureau of Labor Statistics notes that musicians perform in concert halls, clubs, churches, studios, and other settings and may travel locally, nationally, or internationally (Bureau of Labor Statistics). A performer may benefit from living near an active music market or network of venues. My own career also shows how digital work can cross borders. I have roots in Virginia and now live in the Philippines, while Mr. Blindbandit and Blindbandit Records maintain an online presence that can reach beyond one city or country (Mr. Blindbandit). Geography still matters because of time zones, travel costs, and local networks; however, websites, digital distribution, online communication, and remote collaboration make it possible to build parts of a music career from almost anywhere. That flexibility allows my creative work to continue while I build a life in the Philippines.

My experiences are another reason music feels like the right career. Blindness has required me to ask questions, learn unfamiliar systems, and solve practical problems without depending on sight. Those habits are useful in an independent creative business. In August 2024, I traveled alone from the United States to the Philippines, moving through multiple airports with my white cane, screen-reader technology, and assistance when I needed it. I later wrote that I did not confuse independence with pretending I never needed help (Heck). That lesson applies directly to music. An independent artist still needs collaborators, listeners, distributors, technicians, and other professionals; independence means being responsible for the direction of the work, not refusing support. Sound has always carried information for me - voices, movement, distance, emotion, rhythm, and space - so turning sound into creative work connects naturally to skills I have developed throughout my life. Mr. Blindbandit represents a career path built around how I already think, listen, communicate, and create.

I do not romanticize the profession. The Bureau of Labor Statistics identifies dedication, discipline, interpersonal skills, musical talent, stamina, and promotional skills as important qualities for musicians and singers (Bureau of Labor Statistics). Those qualities connect to everyday realities: rejection happens, schedules can be irregular, audiences have endless choices, and an artist may spend as much time promoting a release as creating it. O*NET also emphasizes organization, relationships, problem solving, and keeping knowledge current (O*NET OnLine). I want to keep strengthening those areas by improving my production skills, learning more about the business side of music, building professional relationships, maintaining an accessible website, and treating releases as organized projects. Talent may attract attention, but a sustainable career depends on habits and systems that continue when excitement fades. The creative side and the business side have to work together; ignoring either one makes the career harder to maintain.

A career in music is demanding because it asks one person to be creative, technical, organized, persistent, and public at the same time. Research from the Bureau of Labor Statistics and O*NET shows that musicians must do far more than perform: they practice, record, promote, collaborate, travel, plan, and continually develop their skills. The field does not require one universal degree, but it does require serious training, and its income can be irregular even for skilled professionals. For me, those challenges do not make the career less attractive. They make it a career worth approaching deliberately. My experience as a blind creator has taught me to adapt tools to my needs, to listen closely, and to keep moving when a process is difficult; my work as Mr. Blindbandit and founder of Blindbandit Records gives those abilities a professional direction. I want a career that allows me to create sound, tell stories, build something of my own, and connect with people across borders. Music does all four, which is why it is not only a career I am interested in someday - it is the career I am already learning how to build.
