Argumentative Essay / KAELEB SAVON HECK

The Best Post-Secondary Path Is the One That Fits the Student

An argument for choosing education and training that fits each student’s goals, abilities, accessibility needs and finances.

Author
Kaeleb Savon Heck
School
Penn Foster High School
Course
English
Date
12 September 2026

For generations, students have often heard one basic message after high school: go to college if you want to be successful. College can absolutely be the right choice, but it is not the only path to a meaningful career. Students have different abilities, financial situations, interests, disabilities, and goals. Some careers require a four-year degree, while others can be reached through community college, technical training, apprenticeships, entrepreneurship, or direct work experience. The best post-secondary path for students is therefore not one universal route. Students should choose the education or training that matches their career goals, strengths, accessibility needs, and financial circumstances.

There is strong evidence that higher education can provide major economic benefits. According to the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, workers age twenty-five and older with a bachelor’s degree had median weekly earnings of $1,578 in 2025, compared with $966 for workers whose highest credential was a high school diploma. The unemployment rate was also lower for bachelor’s degree holders, 2.8 percent compared with 4.3 percent for high school graduates (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, “Education Pays”). These numbers are an important argument for college, especially for students pursuing careers that require degrees. A student who wants to become a teacher, engineer, attorney, or physician cannot simply replace the required education with ambition.

However, those statistics do not prove that every student needs the same type of education. The Bureau of Labor Statistics itself separates occupations according to different entry-level requirements, including associate degrees, post-secondary nondegree awards, high school diplomas, and other forms of training (“Employment, Wages”). The U.S. Department of Education also recognizes career pathways that include career and technical education and apprenticeships (“Career Pathways”). These options matter because success should be measured by whether a person gains the skills needed for a chosen career, not by whether everyone earns the same credential.

My own life is one reason I believe this. I was born blind because of Leber congenital amaurosis, and throughout my life people have sometimes assumed that blindness should determine what I can do. I learned the opposite. In 2024, I traveled independently from the United States to the Philippines, using my white cane and airport assistance when necessary. That experience reinforced something I had already learned through music: independence does not mean doing everything in exactly the same way as everyone else. It means finding the tools and pathway that allow you to reach your goal.

Music became one of my pathways. I developed my identity as the artist Mr. Blindbandit and built Blindbandit Records. I have also worked on developing my online presence through MrBlindbandit.net. My path has required creativity, technology, communication, persistence, and learning how the music business works. It has not looked like a traditional four-year college route, but that does not make the skills or work less real. For a blind student, accessibility can also influence which route makes sense. A program may look excellent on paper but become a poor choice if its technology, materials, or training environment is inaccessible. Students with disabilities should be able to consider accessibility as part of choosing a path without being told that their disability makes their ambitions unrealistic.

Critics might argue that encouraging alternatives to four-year college could cause students to settle for less. That concern deserves attention because education is strongly associated with higher earnings and lower unemployment. Yet giving students more choices is not the same as discouraging education. In fact, it requires students to think more carefully about what education they actually need. A future nurse may need a nursing degree, while another student may benefit more from an apprenticeship, a technical credential, an associate degree, or specialized business training. The Department of Education describes career exploration as a way for students to connect their interests and abilities with education and training options, including non-college programs (“Career and Educational Pathways”). The goal should be informed choice rather than automatic enrollment.

Cost also makes this decision important. Spending years in a program that does not connect to a student’s goals can waste both time and money. Students should compare tuition, potential debt, accessibility, expected earnings, job opportunities, and the actual qualifications employers require. The right answer may still be a university degree, but it should be chosen for a reason. For someone else, a shorter credential or work-based program may provide a faster and more practical route into a career.

There is no single post-secondary path that guarantees success. College remains valuable and necessary for many careers, but it should be one option among several respected choices. My experience as a blind person, musician, entrepreneur, and international traveler has taught me that a person’s path does not have to resemble everyone else’s to be legitimate. Students need accurate information, accessible opportunities, and the freedom to choose a route that fits their goals. The best path after high school is not simply the path people traditionally expect. It is the path that gives an individual student the strongest realistic opportunity to build the life and career that student wants.
