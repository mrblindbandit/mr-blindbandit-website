## Range XVI (paragraphs 629–648)

Part XVI - Email, Newsletter, and Electronic Communications Policy

INTERNAL IMPLEMENTATION STANDARD WITH PUBLIC-FACING RULES

1. Marketing consent

Newsletter signup should be separate from account creation, music submission, booking inquiry, and label-portal access. Prechecked boxes should not be used where affirmative consent is required.

2. Consent evidence

Maintain the email address, signup source, timestamp, consent version or language, and confirmation state necessary to demonstrate the signup. Double opt-in is recommended for list quality and evidence even where it is not universally mandatory.

3. Message identification

Commercial email should use accurate sender and routing information, non-deceptive subject lines, a clear identity for the sender, and any advertising identification required by applicable law.

4. Postal address

Where United States CAN-SPAM applies, marketing email should contain a valid physical postal address or another address form accepted by the statute. Use the currently published business address unless a compliant commercial mail receiving or other lawful alternative is later adopted.

5. Unsubscribe

Every marketing email should contain a conspicuous, functional unsubscribe method. Requests should be processed promptly and no later than the period required by applicable law; CAN-SPAM requires honoring covered opt-outs within 10 business days. The unsubscribe mechanism should remain functional for the period required by law.

6. Suppression

A minimal suppression record should remain after unsubscribe so the address is not accidentally re-imported into marketing. Suppression is not the same as continuing to send marketing.

7. Transactional communications

Security alerts, password or sign-in communications, requested receipts, contract notices, submission confirmations, portal notifications, and direct responses can be treated differently from promotional marketing depending on content and law. Do not disguise marketing as a transactional message.

8. Vendor configuration

Resend or any replacement email provider should use authenticated domains, SPF, DKIM, and DMARC appropriate to the sending configuration, role separation for transactional and marketing mail where useful, bounce handling, complaint handling, and rate limits.

## Range XVII (paragraphs 648–671)

Part XVII - Third-Party Services, Open-Source Software, and Vendor Governance

INTERNAL IMPLEMENTATION STANDARD

1. Vendor inventory

Maintain an inventory of providers, purpose, data categories, processing location if known, contract or terms link, privacy terms, security notes, subprocessors where relevant, retention controls, and whether the provider receives public, account, submission, advertising, or portal data.

2. Least disclosure

A vendor should receive only information needed for the configured feature. Avoid transmitting private portal records to public analytics or advertising services. Remove unused SDKs, tags, trackers, and stale integrations.

3. Authentication

Clerk configuration should be reviewed for redirect URIs, allowed origins, session duration, MFA, OAuth scopes, user metadata exposure, webhook verification, secret-key storage, and production/development separation. Secret keys must remain server-side and must never be embedded in public JavaScript.

4. Cloudflare and infrastructure

Cloudflare-backed services should use modern TLS, HTTPS redirection, HSTS after configuration is verified, appropriate caching rules, bot and rate controls where needed, and careful handling of authenticated or private responses so private material is not publicly cached.

5. Google and advertising

AdSense should be restricted to intended public pages. European consent signals, U.S. state privacy messages if used, ad-technology-provider configuration, ads.txt, and privacy messaging should be reviewed periodically against the actual account.

6. Payment providers

PayPal, Wise, or future payment providers should handle payment credentials on their own secure flows wherever practical. The website should avoid collecting complete card or bank credentials unless a compliant payment architecture is intentionally deployed.

7. Embedded media

Spotify, Apple Music, YouTube, and similar embeds should be reviewed for privacy, consent, accessibility, and performance. Provide direct links when embeds create accessibility or privacy barriers.

8. Open-source software bill of materials

Maintain a software bill of materials for client and server dependencies, especially media codecs, parsers, FFmpeg/WebAssembly builds, image libraries, video renderers, authentication packages, and security-sensitive dependencies. Record version, license, source, modification status, and distribution obligations.

9. FFmpeg license review

Before distributing a bundled FFmpeg or FFmpeg-derived WebAssembly build, identify the exact build options and linked libraries. Determine whether the resulting build is under LGPL, GPL, or another combination and satisfy corresponding notices, source availability, relinking, or other obligations. Never publish a generic "FFmpeg is LGPL" statement without checking the actual build.

10. Dependency security

Use automated dependency alerts, lockfiles, integrity controls where supported, reproducible or pinned production builds, routine upgrades, and emergency patch procedures for critical vulnerabilities. Remove abandoned packages when a maintained replacement is feasible.

## Range XVIII (paragraphs 747–770)

Part XVIII - Internal Privacy Rights Request Procedure

INTERNAL OPERATING PROCEDURE - DO NOT PUBLISH AS A SUBSTITUTE FOR THE PUBLIC PRIVACY POLICY

1. Intake

Accept privacy-rights requests at privacy@mrblindbandit.net and from any dedicated privacy form added later. Record the date received, request type, jurisdiction claimed if relevant, affected email or account, requested scope, verification status, deadline, actions taken, exceptions relied upon, response date, and closure status.

2. Minimize identity verification

Ask only for information reasonably necessary to match and authenticate the request. Prefer account sign-in confirmation, verified email control, or information already on file over collection of new government identification. If stronger proof is genuinely required, provide a secure channel and delete unnecessary verification copies after the verification purpose is complete.

3. Request categories

Operational categories should include access/know, copy/portability, correction, deletion/erasure, restriction, objection, withdrawal of consent, marketing opt-out, advertising opt-out where applicable, account closure, authorized-agent request, appeal where applicable, and general privacy inquiry.

4. Search scope

Search relevant systems instead of only the front-end database. Depending on the request, this can include authentication records, public community records, moderation notes, submission records, support email, newsletter records, label portal records, security logs, payment-provider references, vendor-held records under the operator's control, and backups to the extent required and technically reasonable.

5. Exemptions and preservation

Before deleting records, check for contract administration, royalties, accounting or tax duties, fraud prevention, security, intellectual-property disputes, legal holds, active litigation or claims, regulatory obligations, freedom-of-expression considerations, and records necessary to demonstrate prior compliance. Retain only what the lawful exception requires.

6. Vendor requests

When a processor or service provider holds information that must be corrected, exported, or deleted, use the provider's administrative tools or support process and document the action. Do not tell a user data was deleted everywhere until applicable downstream deletion has been completed or a lawful exception is explained.

7. Response quality

Responses should be understandable, accessible, and specific. If a request is denied or limited, explain the reason and available complaint or appeal rights where required. Do not use a vague "security reasons" statement when a more precise explanation can be given safely.

8. Deadlines

The DPO should maintain a jurisdictional deadline tracker rather than assuming every privacy law uses the same response period. Start the internal target substantially before the legal deadline so identity questions and vendor work do not cause a late response.

9. Sensitive exports

Exports containing private information should use a secure delivery method, limited-lived download, authenticated portal, or encrypted archive where appropriate. Do not send a large private-data export to an unverified email address merely because that address appears in a request.

10. Audit trail

Keep a proportionate record of the request and disposition so the operator can demonstrate compliance without retaining an unnecessary duplicate of all information that was deleted.

## Range XIX (paragraphs 770–805)

Part XIX - Internal Data Retention and Deletion Schedule

INTERNAL IMPLEMENTATION TARGETS - VERIFY AGAINST ACTUAL CODE, CONTRACTS, ACCOUNTING, TAX, AND LEGAL DUTIES BEFORE AUTOMATING

1. Governing principle

Do not keep information forever merely because storage is cheap. Each category needs a documented purpose, owner, trigger date, ordinary period, legal-hold override, and deletion or anonymization method. Production code and vendor settings must match the published retention description.

2. Local-only Media Suite source files

Target: no server retention because the source remains in the browser. Revoke browser object URLs and release temporary references after processing or navigation where feasible.

3. Future server-side temporary media

Target: automatic deletion within 24 hours after successful processing or failure unless the user expressly saves the project into an authenticated workspace with a different disclosed lifecycle. Never introduce silent indefinite media storage.

4. Rejected or inactive music-submission media

Target: 12 months after the review closes or becomes inactive, unless the submitter requests earlier deletion and no lawful preservation reason applies. Consider shorter periods for large source audio once review is complete while retaining minimal submission metadata needed to prevent confusion or document decisions.

5. Submission metadata and ordinary professional inquiries

Target: 24 months after closure or last meaningful activity for ordinary unsuccessful inquiries. Active negotiations and accepted relationships move into the applicable contractual record schedule.

6. Community content

Public posts and comments: retain while published and until deleted by the user or moderation process, subject to legal or safety holds. Deleted public content should be removed from ordinary active display promptly. Backend deletion or anonymization should follow the configured lifecycle.

7. Moderation and abuse records

Target: 24 months after final action for ordinary moderation. Serious threats, exploitation, fraud, repeat-infringer evidence, litigation, or regulatory matters may justify longer preservation. Avoid retaining full prohibited content when a hash, URL, decision record, or redacted evidence is sufficient.

8. Authentication and security logs

Target: 12 months for ordinary sign-in, fraud, rate-limit, and security logs unless a shorter provider default is sufficient or a longer period is justified by a documented investigation. High-risk privileged-administration logs may justify a longer controlled period.

9. Support tickets

Target: 24 months after closure for ordinary support. Move contract, safety, dispute, or rights-request records into the applicable longer schedule when necessary.

10. Marketing consent evidence

Target: retain active consent evidence while marketing continues and for a reasonable limitation/compliance period afterward. Keep a minimal unsubscribe/suppression identifier for as long as needed to honor the opt-out and demonstrate compliance.

11. Privacy-rights request records

Target: approximately three years after closure unless applicable law, limitation periods, or enforcement risk justify a different period. Do not retain unnecessary identity documents merely because a rights request occurred.

12. Contracts, royalty, catalog, accounting, payout, and tax records

Target: relationship duration plus at least seven years after the applicable accounting or contractual period where consistent with governing tax, accounting, limitation, distributor, and contract requirements. Some rights and royalty records can require longer preservation because ownership and royalty obligations can outlive ordinary business correspondence.

13. Label portal messages and tasks

Operational notes: review annually and remove stale material that has no continuing business purpose. Messages that form part of contract administration, release instructions, royalty disputes, approvals, or rights evidence should follow the corresponding contract or rights-record schedule.

14. Incident and breach records

Target: at least five years after closure or longer if NPC reporting rules, litigation, insurer requirements, or regulatory action require it. Preserve the incident chronology, decisions, notices, remediation, and evidence without maintaining unnecessary exposed data.

15. Backups

Target: rolling overwrite within 30 to 90 days depending on architecture. Deleted information may remain in secure backups until normal overwrite if restoration procedures prevent ordinary reintroduction or include a process to re-apply deletion after restoration.

16. Legal holds

A documented legal hold overrides ordinary deletion for specifically identified records. Holds should have an owner, scope, reason, start date, periodic review, and release process. Do not freeze unrelated data merely because one dispute exists.

## Range XX (paragraphs 805–826)

Part XX - Internal Security Incident and Personal Data Breach Response Plan

INTERNAL OPERATING PROCEDURE

1. Incident lead

Kaeleb Savon Heck serves as Security and Incident Response Lead and Data Protection Officer for this procedure. Security intake: security@mrblindbandit.net. Privacy escalation: privacy@mrblindbandit.net.

2. Immediate priorities

On receiving a credible report: preserve evidence; prevent further unauthorized access; rotate exposed credentials or keys; isolate affected systems if necessary; preserve logs; identify affected data and users; avoid destroying forensic evidence; and document every material action with timestamps.

3. Severity triage

Classify incidents according to data sensitivity, number of people, active attacker access, credential compromise, privilege level, financial exposure, public availability, child-safety implications, likelihood of identity fraud, operational impact, and whether protected or sensitive personal information was acquired.

4. Vendor incidents

If a provider is involved, open the provider's security process immediately, preserve case numbers and notices, determine what the provider knows, and do not assume the provider will satisfy the operator's own notification obligations. Contractual processor notice obligations should be recorded in the vendor inventory.

5. Philippine breach assessment

Assess whether the incident meets the Philippine mandatory-notification criteria. The NPC framework can require notification to the Commission and affected data subjects within 72 hours of knowledge or reasonable belief of a qualifying personal-data breach. If the incident is not reportable, document the analysis and include the incident in required Annual Security Incident Report processes as applicable.

6. Notification content

Where notice is required, provide clear information about the nature of the incident, affected data, likely consequences, containment and remediation, protective actions individuals can take, and a contact for further information. Do not delay simply to make every fact perfect if the law requires an initial notice based on available information.

7. Credentials and secret exposure

If a Clerk secret, API key, database credential, email key, Cloudflare token, webhook secret, signing key, or other privileged credential is exposed, rotate or revoke it immediately, review logs from before and after rotation, identify unauthorized actions, and remove the secret from repositories, builds, logs, support transcripts, and browser-delivered code where applicable.

8. User credential compromise

Invalidate sessions where appropriate, require credential reset or reauthentication, notify affected users when warranted, and review account changes, email changes, MFA changes, role grants, document access, and exports.

9. Post-incident review

After containment, record root cause, control failures, impact, notification decisions, remediation, owner, due dates, and evidence that the fix was deployed. Update threat models, tests, policies, or training so the same failure is less likely to recur.

## Range XXI (paragraphs 826–845)

Part XXI - Internal Moderation, Illegal-Content, and Online-Safety Procedure

INTERNAL OPERATING PROCEDURE

1. Reporting channel

Provide a user-friendly method to report a specific post, comment, account, or other item. The report should capture the URL or content identifier, reason, supporting explanation, reporter contact where appropriate, urgency, and category without forcing the reporter to send unrelated private data.

2. Categories

Use consistent moderation categories such as copyright, impersonation, privacy/doxxing, harassment, threat/violence, child safety, sexual exploitation, fraud/scam, malware/security, illegal goods/services, spam/manipulation, hate/dehumanization, self-harm encouragement, non-consensual intimate content, and other legal concern.

3. Urgent escalation

Threats to life, suspected child sexual exploitation, active credential theft, malware distribution, and exposure of highly sensitive personal information should receive priority review. Preserve necessary evidence while minimizing additional exposure.

4. Decision record

For a meaningful restriction, record the content identifier, policy or legal basis, evidence reviewed, action, duration, reviewer, automation used if relevant, date, user notice, appeal availability, and appeal result.

5. Reasons to users

When appropriate and safe, tell the affected user what rule or legal issue caused the action and what content was affected. Do not disclose a reporter's private identity or law-enforcement-sensitive information merely to explain the decision.

6. Appeals

Provide a meaningful appeal path for significant content or account restrictions unless doing so would be unlawful, unsafe, or obviously abusive. A reviewer should examine context and evidence rather than treating the original action as automatically correct.

7. DSA readiness if scope applies

If the service becomes subject to the EU Digital Services Act as a hosting service or online platform, verify the exact obligations that apply to its size and establishment/targeting facts. Build the report form so it can support Article 16-style sufficiently precise notices and build the moderation system so qualifying restrictions can generate Article 17-style statements of reasons. Do not claim DSA compliance before scope and implementation are confirmed.

8. UK Online Safety readiness if scope applies

For a user-to-user service within UK Online Safety Act scope, maintain an illegal-content risk assessment, relevant safety measures, reporting and complaints processes, and terms describing protections. Complete and document a children's access assessment; if children are likely to access the service, complete the required child-risk and protection work. Scope and duties must be reassessed as the community changes.

Add an open-source notices page or bundled notices file where needed.

Pin dependency versions and verify subresource integrity when appropriate and compatible with the delivery architecture.

Phase 15 - Community moderation and online safety

Put a Report action on each post/comment where possible.

Build a report queue with category, content ID, urgency, reporter contact, timestamps, reviewer, action, reason, and appeal.

Build user-facing block/mute tools if community features justify them.

Add rate limits for posts, comments, votes, reports, and account creation.

Add spam detection without making automated scores the only basis for severe decisions.

Create severe-content escalation for threats, child exploitation, credential theft, malware, and doxxing.

Build an appeal path for significant actions.

Preserve moderation reason codes and notices so future DSA/UK assessments are easier if scope applies.

Phase 16 - Accessibility engineering baseline

Set WCAG 2.2 Level AA as the development target.

Add eslint-plugin-jsx-a11y or the equivalent lint tooling if the stack supports it.

Add axe-core automated tests to key pages and components.

Add Playwright or equivalent keyboard tests for menus, dialogs, forms, community posting, authentication, and Media Suite flows.

Use Lighthouse only as a secondary automated signal, never as proof of WCAG conformance.

Record accessibility defects in the ordinary issue tracker with severity and regression tests.

Phase 17 - Menu and modal code requirements

Use a real button for the menu trigger with aria-expanded and aria-controls.

Give the menu/dialog an accessible name.

On open, move focus to a sensible first element or dialog container.

Constrain Tab/Shift+Tab to an active modal when the background must be unavailable.

Make the background inert or otherwise remove it from the interactive focus order while the modal is active.

Close on Escape when the pattern permits it.

Return focus to the trigger after close.

Provide a clearly named Close button with an adequate target size.

Prevent background scrolling when appropriate without trapping screen-reader virtual navigation incorrectly.

Test the exact VoiceOver gesture flow, including moving to blank space, dialog boundaries, and dismissal behavior requested by the operator.

Phase 18 - Form accessibility code requirements

Associate every input with a label element or equivalent programmatic label.

Use autocomplete tokens for common identity/contact fields where appropriate.

Expose required state programmatically.

On validation failure, provide an error summary linking to invalid fields and set aria-invalid where appropriate.

Use role=status or aria-live=polite for non-urgent async completion messages; reserve assertive announcements for urgent errors.

Do not disable the submit button in a way that prevents the user from learning why submission is unavailable.

Phase 19 - Media Suite accessibility code requirements

Every visual timeline must have start/end time inputs that work by keyboard and screen reader.

Every drag handle must have a non-drag alternative.

Waveform selection must expose selected times numerically.

Loudness meters must expose numeric LUFS and peak values.

Normalization presets must be ordinary radio buttons, select options, or buttons with clear selected state.

Progress must expose current stage and percentage when meaningful.

Canvas previews must have an adjacent accessible control panel and text description of current project state.

Artwork dimensions and crop values must be editable in labeled fields.

Aspect-ratio lock must expose on/off state.

Caption and lyric timing tools must provide a text-list editor in addition to any visual timeline.

Export success must announce filename, format, and completion without forcing focus unexpectedly.

Phase 20 - Accessibility visual and responsive checks

Verify normal text contrast of at least the applicable WCAG AA ratio and UI/component contrast where required.

Verify focus indicators remain visible in default and high-contrast modes.

Test browser zoom to 200 percent.

Test reflow at a 320 CSS-pixel-wide viewport or equivalent 400-percent zoom condition required by WCAG testing.

Test landscape and portrait phone layouts.

Test prefers-reduced-motion.

Ensure the persistent Listen drawer, consent UI, sticky footer, or cookie controls never completely obscure keyboard focus.

Check that touch targets satisfy WCAG 2.2 target-size rules or valid exceptions.

Phase 21 - Media accessibility

Provide transcripts for audio-only content where required and useful.

Provide audio description or equivalent access for important visual-only information where required.

Do not autoplay audio unexpectedly.

Phase 22 - Language and translation

Set the correct html lang value for each first-party language version.

When switching first-party languages dynamically, update the document language.

Keep private portal data out of third-party automatic translation unless the privacy/security implications have been intentionally approved.

Phase 23 - Pre-launch legal truth test

If it cannot be verified, either implement it or soften the statement before publishing.

Phase 24 - Regression and monitoring

Re-run legal-page diff checks whenever a feature begins collecting a new category of information.

Schedule quarterly policy-to-product review and annual full compliance audit.

## Range XXIII (paragraphs 1055–1364)

Part XXIII - WCAG 2.2 Level AA Accessibility Audit, Remediation Standard, and Acceptance Plan

INTERNAL AUDIT AND ENGINEERING STANDARD. The public Accessibility Statement is in Part XIV. This Part is the detailed test plan Work should use against production.

1. Standard and scope

The accessibility target for mrblindbandit.net is WCAG 2.2 Level AA across the public website, authentication, community, forms, consent interfaces, Media Suite, and private label portal to the extent each surface is under the operator's control.

The requested standard is WCAG 2.2 AA. There is no "GAFC 2.8" web accessibility standard. WCAG 2.2 is the current W3C Recommendation used by this package. Where a jurisdiction uses WCAG 2.1 AA or another incorporated technical standard, targeting WCAG 2.2 AA generally adds newer requirements rather than removing the older Level A and AA criteria, but legal scope must still be assessed separately.

Conformance cannot be proven by a scanner, Lighthouse score, accessibility overlay, or policy statement. Work must combine source-code review, automated testing, keyboard testing, visual review, browser zoom/reflow testing, and screen-reader testing.

2. Live-site positives observed in the September 13, 2026 review

The current public site already has several useful foundations:

A Skip to content mechanism is exposed on public pages.

The site has an Accessibility page and user-facing larger-text, high-contrast, and pause-motion controls.

Public legal and safety pages are reachable from the site footer.

Current public forms expose visible field labels in the text representation reviewed.

The public Privacy Policy explains authentication, community information, submissions, advertising, and local-browser conversion.

Public pages use headings and generally descriptive link text rather than being one undifferentiated block.

The website has dedicated role addresses such as privacy, copyright, safety, security, and accessibility contacts.

The site already treats accessibility as a product concern rather than relying solely on an accessibility widget.

These positives are not a conformance certification. The remaining acceptance tests below are required.

3. Live-site issues or inconsistencies to remediate

Work should treat the following as concrete review items rather than theoretical suggestions:

1. The live Home experience has used wording such as "Sign in with ChatGPT to post" while the Privacy Policy describes Clerk as the authentication provider. The visible product copy and actual authentication implementation must agree. If Clerk is the real provider, do not present another service as the login mechanism unless that service is genuinely an enabled identity provider and the wording is technically accurate.

2. The Accessibility page currently routes reports through a general business address in at least one place while a dedicated accessibility@mrblindbandit.net address exists elsewhere. Standardize accessibility feedback on accessibility@mrblindbandit.net and keep a general route as fallback only.

3. A contact-form honeypot has appeared in extracted page text as "Leave this field empty." Verify that the anti-spam field is removed from the accessibility tree and normal keyboard order when it is intended to be invisible. Do not use aria-hidden on a focusable input; the control itself should be truly hidden/non-focusable or otherwise implemented accessibly.

4. A persistent Listen drawer and its Close control appear in page extraction. Verify that when the drawer is closed, its hidden controls are not keyboard-focusable or exposed as active controls to assistive technology. When it opens, announce the state appropriately without unnecessarily trapping reading navigation.

5. The Menu button must expose expanded/collapsed state and its controlled region programmatically. Verify aria-expanded changes correctly and aria-controls points to the actual menu container.

6. Larger Text, High Contrast, and Pause Motion controls should expose their current on/off state with a native control or an appropriate state such as aria-pressed. Their labels must remain understandable without relying on icon shape or color.

7. Google consent and Privacy Choices interfaces must be tested as real interactive components. A consent interface that is legally correct but inaccessible is still a product failure.

8. Embedded third-party players need an accessible first-party link or alternate path when the embed creates a barrier.

9. Every Media Suite visual control requires a nonvisual, keyboard-operable equivalent. This is a launch blocker for a suite intended to be usable by its blind owner.

4. Priority severity model

Work should assign every accessibility defect one of four severities.

Critical means a user cannot complete a core task at all, such as signing in, accepting or refusing consent, posting, submitting music, uploading a file, choosing a Media Suite preset, exporting a file, or opening the label portal without sight or a mouse.

High means the task can technically be completed but only with severe confusion, an undocumented workaround, inaccessible CAPTCHA, keyboard trap, lost focus, unlabeled control, missing error association, or another major barrier.

Medium means the task is usable but violates a WCAG requirement or causes repeated friction, such as weak focus styling, inconsistent headings, redundant announcements, small targets, or avoidable horizontal scrolling.

Low means a best-practice or usability defect that does not independently block WCAG AA conformance but should still be corrected.

Critical and High defects on core flows should block launch of the affected feature.

5. Global semantic structure acceptance tests

For every first-party page, Work should verify:

The document has one useful page title that identifies both the page and site where practical.

The primary language is set on the html element.

There is a single meaningful main landmark for the main content.

Header, navigation, main, complementary content, and footer use native landmarks where appropriate.

A keyboard user can bypass repeated navigation using the skip link or an equivalent mechanism.

Heading levels communicate structure and do not exist solely to make text visually large.

Heading order is understandable when navigating only by heading commands in VoiceOver or NVDA.

Lists use list semantics when content is actually a list.

Buttons are buttons and links are links. A clickable div should not impersonate a button when native HTML can perform the job.

Decorative icons are hidden from assistive technology and do not create noisy spoken labels.

Icon-only actionable controls have concise accessible names.

Duplicate IDs, broken label references, and invalid ARIA relationships are removed.

ARIA is not used to override correct native semantics unnecessarily.

6. Keyboard-only acceptance tests

A tester must be able to load the page, put the mouse aside, and complete every core task using Tab, Shift+Tab, Enter, Space, arrow keys where expected, Escape, and ordinary text editing keys.

Verify that:

All interactive controls are reachable in a logical order.

No visible interactive control is pointer-only.

No focusable element is invisible or intentionally hidden.

Focus never becomes trapped except inside a genuinely modal interface that provides a clear exit.

Escape closes dismissible menus and dialogs when that is the expected interaction.

Closing a modal restores focus to the logical triggering control unless the workflow intentionally moves the user elsewhere.

Custom menus do not require undocumented keyboard commands.

Keyboard activation performs the same essential action as pointer activation.

Repeated tabbing does not unexpectedly move focus to background controls behind an open dialog.

Sticky banners, drawers, headers, consent messages, and floating controls do not completely obscure the focused element.

7. VoiceOver acceptance tests on iPhone and Safari

Because the operator uses iPhone VoiceOver, this is a mandatory first-class test rather than an optional compatibility check.

For each core flow:

Navigate by headings and confirm the page outline is sensible.

Navigate by links and confirm each spoken label makes sense out of context.

Navigate by form controls and confirm labels, values, required state, errors, descriptions, and selection state are spoken.

Open the Menu and verify VoiceOver announces it as a button with expanded/collapsed state.

Open every custom dialog or popup and verify the dialog has a name, focus moves into it appropriately, and dismissal is discoverable.

Use the rotor to navigate form controls, headings, links, landmarks, and errors where relevant.

Verify toggles expose selected/on/off state.

Verify live updates such as "upload complete," "conversion 70 percent," "export ready," "comment posted," or "error" are announced once and at an appropriate urgency.

Verify hidden drawers, closed menus, and inactive modals do not remain reachable by swiping.

Verify a user can move to a logical blank/background area and dismiss a popover where that interaction is intentionally provided, while also retaining a conventional Close button.

Verify swipe order follows reading and visual order closely enough to avoid confusion.

Verify no gesture unique to sighted dragging is required for a Media Suite task.

8. Desktop screen-reader acceptance tests

Use macOS VoiceOver with Safari and, where feasible, NVDA with a current Chromium or Firefox browser.

At minimum, test Home, Music, Videos, Bio, Contact, Privacy, Terms, Community, authentication, a post/comment flow, music submission, every Media Suite tool, Privacy Choices, and the private portal's primary dashboard flow.

The purpose is not to make every screen reader speak identically. The purpose is to confirm that native semantics and accessibility APIs expose an equivalent task and understandable state.

9. Focus visibility and WCAG 2.2 Focus Not Obscured

The focused element must remain visibly identifiable. Work should test this with:

The normal header and footer.

The Listen drawer.

Cookie/CMP messages.

Accessibility controls.

Sticky toolbars.

Mobile browser chrome.

Media Suite transport controls.

Any floating chat, help, or share controls.

At Level AA, WCAG 2.2 includes Focus Not Obscured (Minimum). If a sticky element covers the focused field or button completely, fix the layout, scroll margin, overlay position, or focus behavior instead of expecting the user to manually scroll around it.

10. Target Size (Minimum)

WCAG 2.2 Level AA includes Target Size (Minimum). Review small close icons, menu controls, transport controls, waveform handles, drag handles, vote icons, pagination, checkboxes, tiny social icons, and inline edit controls.

Use sufficiently large targets or valid spacing/exception logic. Work should not "pass" a 12-pixel icon just because it visually looks stylish. Increase the clickable area without necessarily increasing the visible icon.

11. Dragging Movements

WCAG 2.2 Level AA requires a non-drag method when dragging is used and dragging is not essential.

For the Media Suite:

A clip range must have start-time and end-time fields.

A crop box must have numeric X/Y/width/height or equivalent controls.

Artwork positioning must have keyboard controls or numeric position fields.

Waveform selection must have time inputs.

Timeline markers must be editable without dragging.

Slider values must have keyboard support and a spoken numeric value.

Caption or lyric timing must have a text-list editor with timecode fields.

A blind user must be able to perform the same essential edit without interpreting a visual waveform.

12. Accessible Authentication (Minimum)

WCAG 2.2 Level AA adds Accessible Authentication (Minimum). Authentication should not depend on solving, memorizing, or transcribing a cognitive-function test unless an allowed alternative or assistance mechanism exists.

Work should:

Allow password managers.

Allow paste into password and one-time-code fields unless a specific security requirement makes it genuinely necessary to restrict it.

Use autocomplete tokens where appropriate.

Give one-time-code fields clear labels and instructions.

Ensure social sign-in buttons have accurate provider names.

Avoid visual-only CAPTCHA. If bot protection is used, provide an accessible path.

Keep authentication errors specific enough to be useful without leaking unnecessary security information.

Test Clerk components with screen readers instead of assuming vendor components are automatically accessible.

13. Consistent Help and Redundant Entry

WCAG 2.2 adds Consistent Help and Redundant Entry criteria.

If help/contact mechanisms repeat across a set of pages, keep them in a consistent relative order where the criterion applies. Do not repeatedly force a user to re-enter information already supplied in the same process when it can be safely auto-populated or selected, subject to exceptions for security or essential confirmation.

This is particularly relevant to multi-step submissions, account setup, checkout, and label onboarding.

14. Forms, labels, instructions, and errors

Every form must have:

A visible or otherwise properly associated accessible label for every user-editable field.

Programmatic required state.

Clear format instructions before an error occurs when format is unusual.

Autocomplete metadata for common personal fields where appropriate.

Errors in text, not color alone.

aria-invalid or native invalid state where appropriate.

A relationship between the field and its error/help text.

An error summary for long forms when several fields fail.

Focus management that helps the user reach the first meaningful error without causing an unexpected context change.

Success confirmation that is exposed to assistive technology.

For the honeypot anti-spam field, use a method that does not cause a screen-reader user to encounter a fake instruction to leave a mysterious field blank.

15. File upload accessibility

Every upload component should announce or expose:

What file type is expected.

Accepted formats.

Maximum size when one exists.

Whether multiple files are allowed.

The selected filename.

Upload or processing state.

Progress when meaningful.

Validation failure and how to correct it.

Successful completion.

A way to remove or replace the selected file by keyboard.

Native file input controls should be preserved or wrapped in a way that does not destroy their accessibility.

16. Dynamic status and live regions

Use live regions sparingly and intentionally.

Use polite status for ordinary progress, save confirmation, processing completion, or non-urgent updates. Use assertive alerts for urgent errors that require immediate attention. Do not place an entire frequently changing page region inside aria-live because that can cause constant interruption.

For long media jobs, announce major phases and meaningful percentage changes rather than every fraction of a second.

17. Modal dialogs, popovers, and drawers

For a true modal dialog:

Give it an accessible name.

Move focus into the dialog on open.

Ensure all required actions are inside the dialog.

Keep keyboard focus within the modal while it is active.

Make the background inert or otherwise unavailable for interaction.

Provide a clearly named Close or Cancel mechanism where dismissal is allowed.

Support Escape where appropriate.

Restore focus logically on close.

Do not mark a dialog aria-modal="true" if users can still interact with background content.

For nonmodal popovers or drawers, do not apply modal semantics incorrectly. The chosen interaction must match what the UI actually does.

18. Navigation menus

The mobile menu trigger should be a native button. It needs a stable accessible name such as "Menu" and state via aria-expanded. If it controls a specific element, aria-controls should point to that element.

When the menu opens, Work should verify whether focus should move into it based on the implementation. Closing must be possible by keyboard and screen reader. Hidden navigation links must not remain tabbable.

19. Accessibility preference controls

Larger Text, High Contrast, and Pause Motion are useful enhancements. They must not compensate for an inaccessible default experience.

Each control should expose its state and persist only through an appropriate first-party preference mechanism. Changes should take effect without unexpectedly moving focus. High-contrast mode must not erase focus rings, disabled states, links, or errors. Larger text must not cause clipping. Pause Motion must suppress non-essential animation consistently.

20. Text resizing and zoom

At 200 percent text resizing, content and controls must remain usable without lost text, overlapping labels, inaccessible controls, or clipped dialogs except where a valid exception applies.

Browser zoom should also be tested at 200 and 400 percent. The site must not disable pinch zoom or user scaling without an exceptional reason.

21. Reflow

Test at a viewport equivalent to 320 CSS pixels wide for ordinary vertical content. Users should not need horizontal and vertical scrolling together merely to read text or operate forms, except for content where two-dimensional layout is essential.

Media Suite editors can use complex workspaces, but essential actions and values still need a reflow-friendly or alternate control view.

22. Orientation

Do not lock the site to portrait or landscape unless a particular orientation is essential. Test phone layouts in both orientations, including consent messages and Media Suite dialogs.

23. Color and contrast

Work should measure, not eyeball, contrast.

Verify applicable WCAG AA contrast for text and meaningful UI components. Check default mode, high-contrast mode, hover, focus, disabled appearance, visited links if styled, validation errors, placeholders where they carry useful supplemental information, waveform control states, selected presets, and text over artwork.

Color cannot be the only signal for selected, error, success, required, online/offline, or waveform marker state.

24. Links and controls

Avoid generic repeated links such as "click here" where context is insufficient. Social icon links need provider names. External-link icons should not cause a screen reader to repeat meaningless symbol names.

A control that changes application state should usually be a button. A control that navigates to another location should usually be a link.

25. Images and artwork

Informative images need concise alternative text communicating their purpose. Decorative imagery should use empty alt text or an equivalent method so it is ignored.

Album artwork can usually use an alternative such as "Cover art for [release title] by [artist]" unless the artwork itself conveys additional information important to the page.

Do not automatically generate overly long AI descriptions for every decorative image and call that accessibility. Alternative text should match context and purpose.

26. Audio and video accessibility

Prerecorded video with meaningful spoken content needs captions when WCAG requires them. Captions must identify important non-speech audio when needed for understanding. Audio-only material needs a text alternative where required. Important visual information in video needs audio description or another allowed equivalent when the applicable criterion requires it.

The player itself must be keyboard operable. Play, pause, mute, volume, seek, captions, fullscreen, and other first-party controls require accessible names and state. Avoid autoplay with unexpected sound.

27. Media Suite architecture: common shell

Every Media Suite tool should use a consistent structure:

1. Tool title and one-sentence purpose.

2. Source-file selector with accepted formats and size information.

3. Project or source-file summary.

4. Settings grouped under real fieldset/legend or headings.

5. Presets with explicit selected state.

6. Custom controls.

7. Accessible preview/status information.

8. Process/render action.

9. Progress/status region.

10. Output summary including filename, format, size when available, duration, relevant quality information, and a conventional download control.

The shell should keep control order consistent across converter, normalizer, clipper, audiogram, art-track, and other tools.

28. Audio converter accessibility

The converter should expose:

Source filename, media type, duration where available, and source technical details in text.

Output format as a native select, radio group, or equivalent accessible widget.

Professional presets with clear names and descriptions.

Sample rate, bit depth, bitrate, channels, and codec controls when custom mode is used.

Warnings when an option is not supported by the selected container or browser engine.

A clear distinction between lossless, lossy, high-resolution-capable, and upsampled output.

Progress and final technical summary.

Do not communicate "best," "professional," or "distribution-ready" solely by visual badge; include text explaining what the preset actually changes.

29. Loudness analyzer and normalizer accessibility

Expose integrated loudness, true peak or peak measurement, and any other reported metric as text with units. If a meter uses color bands, the same state must be available as text.

Normalization targets should be selectable by keyboard. A preset such as a streaming-platform target must be described as a technical target, not guaranteed acceptance by that platform. If applying gain can clip or require limiting, explain the result in text.

30. Clipper and silence trimmer accessibility

Provide editable start and end times, selected duration, and keyboard commands. Waveform clicking or dragging may be optional convenience, never the only method.

A silence detector should list detected regions or proposed cuts in text so a screen-reader user can inspect, include, exclude, or adjust them.

31. Metadata editor accessibility

Metadata fields should be ordinary labeled form controls. Artwork replacement should announce the selected image and dimensions. Unsupported tags should be clearly identified. If the tool shows before/after metadata, it should be navigable as text rather than a visual-only diff.

32. Art-track generator accessibility

The art-track generator should provide text controls for audio selection, artwork, title, artist, label text, copyright line, resolution, frame rate, duration behavior, background treatment, and output format.

Artwork position/crop must have non-drag inputs. A blind user should be able to produce a conventional centered-artwork art track without using a visual canvas. If optional visual positioning is used, expose numeric coordinates or presets such as Center, Fit, Fill, Top, Bottom, Left, and Right.

The interface must make clear that generating an art-track video does not directly deliver it to YouTube Music, create a YouTube Art Track object, enroll it in Content ID, or create DDEX delivery unless a separate approved partner integration actually performs those functions.

33. Audiogram generator accessibility

Waveform style, caption style, artwork placement, canvas size, progress bar, text, and branding should be selectable with conventional controls. Generated captions need an editable transcript/timecode list. The user needs a text summary of the configured layout.

34. Lyric-video generator accessibility

Lyrics should be editable in a linear text/timecode interface. Visual timeline editing can exist as an enhancement. Each lyric line should have an accessible start time and optional end time. Provide keyboard methods to insert, delete, reorder, and adjust cues.

35. Artwork resizer and platform-pack accessibility

Provide width and height fields, aspect-ratio lock state, crop mode, quality, output format, and preset dimensions in text. A batch export should announce how many files will be generated and then list the results by filename and dimension.

36. Waveform image generator accessibility

A waveform image is itself visual output. The tool still needs accessible configuration and should expose the underlying duration, selected range, amplitude scaling choice, dimensions, and output state in text. Decorative waveform preview canvases can use an accessible text summary rather than pretending every pixel needs an alternative description.

37. Progress, cancellation, and failure recovery

A long-running conversion or render should identify that work is in progress and whether the user can safely cancel. If cancellation leaves no usable output, say so. If an operation fails, preserve the selected settings where safe so a screen-reader user does not have to rebuild the entire project.

Do not use a spinner with no text as the only status signal.

38. Browser-engine loading state

The existing "Loading engine" experience must not be allowed to sit permanently at zero percent with no explanation. Work should add:

A status message saying what component is loading.

A timeout or failure state.

Retry control.

Diagnostic guidance that does not require developer tools.

A fallback path or explanation when the browser cannot load WebAssembly or the CDN asset.

A status announcement to screen readers.

If FFmpeg assets are downloaded from jsDelivr, a network/CSP failure should result in a useful error rather than an endless loading state.

39. Consent and privacy-message accessibility

Test Google's CMP on iPhone VoiceOver, keyboard-only desktop, zoomed desktop, and narrow mobile layout.

The user must be able to locate and operate Consent, Do not consent/Reject, and Manage options where configured. Focus must be visible and logical. The user must also be able to reopen the privacy choices later.

Do not add a second home-built overlay that conflicts with Google's consent state unless Work can prove the two systems synchronize correctly.

40. Community accessibility

The community must support:

Accessible sign-in.

A labeled post composer.

Clear character/attachment rules.

Accessible post actions.

Vote/poll state conveyed without color alone.

Comments in a logical reading order.

Report and block controls with descriptive names.

Moderation notices that explain the affected content and available next action.

Accessible pagination or load-more behavior.

No infinite-scroll design that makes footer/legal links effectively unreachable without an alternative.

If new posts load dynamically, avoid repeatedly moving or resetting the user's screen-reader position.

41. Label portal accessibility

The private portal should meet the same core target. High-priority checks include dashboard headings, contracts, earnings summaries, messages, account roles, file downloads, document review, notices, backup codes, password reset, and any future electronic-signature workflow.

Financial information should be presented as real text, not an inaccessible chart alone. Charts can supplement a text summary and accessible underlying values.

42. Document accessibility

Any PDFs, Word files, statements, contracts, invoices, or downloadable reports generated by the portal should have a document accessibility workflow. A WCAG-compliant web page does not make an inaccessible PDF compliant.

For PDF deliverables, use tagged structure, correct reading order, document language, useful title, headings, text alternatives, labeled links, and form accessibility as applicable. For DOCX files, use actual heading styles, lists, meaningful links, alt text, and logical reading order.

43. Reduced motion and animation

Honor prefers-reduced-motion for decorative or non-essential animation. The site's Pause Motion setting can offer an additional user preference. Avoid flashing content above accessibility thresholds. Animated waveform, progress, hover, and page transitions should not be required to understand state.

44. Time limits

If any feature introduces a time limit for authentication, upload, checkout, editing, or moderation response, identify whether the user can turn it off, adjust it, or extend it where WCAG requires. Security session expiry can have exceptions, but warning and recovery should still be accessible.

45. Accessible names and visible labels

Where a visible label exists, the accessible name should generally include the same words so speech-input and screen-reader users can correlate what they hear with what is displayed. Avoid a button visually labelled "Upload audio" whose accessible name is unrelated such as "Choose asset object."

46. Status after route changes in a single-page application

If the site uses client-side routing, update the document title and move or announce context appropriately after navigation. A screen reader should not remain on an old footer control with no indication that an entirely new page loaded.

47. Error prevention for important transactions

For legal, financial, or destructive actions such as deleting an account, publishing a post, executing a contract, changing payout information, or buying a paid service, apply the applicable WCAG error-prevention requirements. Provide confirmation, review, or reversibility as appropriate.

48. Automated testing suite

Work should add automated accessibility tests that cover representative routes and states. At minimum:

axe-core or equivalent automated rules on core routes.

jsx-a11y or equivalent linting where the framework supports it.

Playwright keyboard smoke tests.

Tests for dialog focus entry/exit.

Tests that menu aria-expanded changes.

Tests that hidden panels are not tabbable.

Tests that form errors connect to fields.

Tests that status messages appear in a live region where intended.

Tests that Media Suite non-drag controls exist.

Automated tests should fail CI on newly introduced serious violations but should not be labelled "WCAG certified."

49. Manual visual testing suite

For every template and custom tool, manually test:

100, 200, and 400 percent browser zoom.

200 percent text-only enlargement where supported.

Narrow reflow.

Light/dark or site appearance variants if offered.

High Contrast preference.

System forced-colors/high-contrast mode where feasible.

Reduced motion.

Long translated strings.

Large validation errors.

No-image or failed-image conditions.

Slow network and failed third-party script conditions.

50. Accessibility evidence file

Maintain a private accessibility evidence record containing the date, production commit/deployment identifier, routes tested, tools and versions, browsers, screen readers, defects, remediation commits, retest results, and known third-party limitations.

A public Accessibility Statement can accurately describe the target, feedback route, and known limitations without claiming a third-party certification that has not occurred.

## Range XXIV (paragraphs 1364–1493)

Part XXIV - Conditional Jurisdiction and Feature Launch Gates

INTERNAL LEGAL SCOPE CHECKLIST. These items are conditional. Work should not publish unsupported statements that a law applies or does not apply; it should evaluate the facts at each launch or major expansion.

1. Philippines Data Privacy Act and National Privacy Commission

End of Master Package

This package intentionally separates public copy from private implementation and filing work. The public website should contain clean policy language and accurate officer/contact information. Registration mechanics, secret values, internal incident procedures, vendor review, test evidence, and Work-mode engineering tasks belong in private records.

The final standard is simple: do not publish a promise the code or operating process cannot keep; do not call the site fully accessible until the core production flows have actually passed WCAG 2.2 AA-oriented manual and automated acceptance testing; and do not let a legal page substitute for consent controls, security controls, retention behavior, moderation operations, or required external filings.
